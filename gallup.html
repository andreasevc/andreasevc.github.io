Profile Map (PCA)

Two-dimensional projection of each person's full 34-talent rank profile, computed via principal component analysis. Points that sit close together have similar strength profiles overall; distance reflects difference across all talents - not just top 5. Each dot is colored by its owner's dominant top-5 domain; my own dot (User33) is outlined. The dashed black ring marks the world average — the Gallup population baseline talent ordering — projected onto the same axes for reference (it does not influence the PCA itself).

What is PCA, and what am I looking at?

Each person in this analysis is described by 34 numbers (their talents ranked 1 - most significant to 34 - least significant). That's a 34-dimensional profile, which is impossible to plot directly. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a technique that squashes high-dimensional data down to 2D so we can actually see it.

The trick: instead of just picking two talents and ignoring the other 32, PCA finds the two directions through the full 34-dimensional space where people differ from each other the most, and projects everyone onto those two directions. Think of it like photographing a 3D sculpture - you can only capture 2D in a photo, so you'd choose the angle that reveals the most interesting structure. PCA picks that "best angle" mathematically, but in 34 dimensions instead of 3.

What this means for the chart below:

  • The axes don't represent any single talent - they're abstract combinations of all 34, weighted to maximize how spread out people appear.
  • Close dots = people with overall similar talent profiles, even if their top-5 lists look different on paper.
  • Distant dots = genuinely different ways of thinking and working.
  • Clusters hint at groups of people who'd likely approach problems in similar ways.
  • The World avg marker (dashed black ring) is the Gallup population baseline talent ordering projected onto these same axes. It is not part of the PCA calculation — PCA still runs only on the 23 collected results — but plotting it here lets us see where a "typical" profile lands relative to the team. People sitting far from the World avg marker have profiles that diverge most from the global baseline.

What Drives the Map

How to read this chart and what positive/negative bars say about a person's position on the map

The Profile Map above places each person on two axes - PC1 (horizontal) and PC2 (vertical) - but the map alone doesn't tell you what those axes mean. This chart breaks each axis down talent by talent: how strongly does ranking each of the 34 talents high push someone toward one end of that PC or the other?

Bars to the right of the centre line are positive contributions; bars to the left are negative. A long bar means the talent is a defining feature of that axis; a tiny bar means it barely moves it.

How to read a person's position on the map using this chart:

  • A person on the far right of the Profile Map is someone whose top talents tend to be the ones at the top of the PC1 column here (talents with positive bars), and whose bottom talents are the ones at the bottom of the column (talents with negative bars).
  • A person on the far left is the opposite - they rank highly the talents that sit at the bottom of the PC1 column (negative bars).
  • The same logic applies vertically: someone at the top of the map ranks highly the talents at the top of the PC2 column; someone at the bottom of the map ranks highly the talents at the bottom of the PC2 column.
  • Two people land close together on the map when they rank the same talents highly - even if their top-5 lists look different on paper, the weighted sum across all 34 talents comes out similar.

Bars are coloured by domain, so if one domain dominates the top of a column and another dominates the bottom, that PC essentially contrasts those two domains. For example, green Strategic Thinking at the top of PC1 and orange Influencing at the bottom would mean PC1 separates "analytical" profiles (right of the map) from "people-driven" ones (left of the map).

Person × Person Similarity

How the matrix works and what "correlation" actually means here

For every pair of people, this matrix asks a single question: how similar are their full 34-talent rankings? The colour of each cell is a Spearman rank correlation, a number between −1 and +1.

Imagine handing two colleagues the same 34 cards (one per talent) and asking each to stack them from "most like me" on top down to "least like me" at the bottom:

  • If both stacks come out in exactly the same order, their correlation is +1 (teal) - effectively the same strengths profile.
  • If one stack is the mirror image of the other - the cards on top of one sit at the bottom of the other - the correlation is −1 (orange).
  • Cells near 0 (white) mean the two orderings are unrelated; knowing one person's ranking tells you almost nothing about the other's.

Rows and columns are ordered by team with separators between teams. Bright teal blocks along a team's diagonal block would mean its members are unusually similar to each other; an isolated bright cell outside the diagonal blocks means two people on different teams share a profile. The matrix diagonal itself is left blank - every person trivially correlates +1 with themselves, which isn't informative.

Talent Co-occurrence

How to read this matrix and what the patterns reveal

For every pair of talents, this matrix counts how many of the 23 people carry both in their top 5. Darker cells = more people share that pair of talents.

Talents are grouped into the four domain blocks, with separators drawn between blocks. There are two patterns worth scanning for:

  • Bright squares clustered inside a block mean talents in that domain tend to be carried by the same people - the domain has internal coherence.
  • Bright cells between blocks (off-domain) reveal interesting cross-domain co-occurrences - e.g. a talent from Executing that often shows up alongside one from Strategic Thinking in the same person's top 5.

The diagonal repeats each talent's overall frequency for reference (a talent always co-occurs with itself, by definition), and is styled with the same scale so you can compare the most-shared pairs against the most-popular individual talents.

Coverage Gaps

Talents that no one in the department has in their top 5 - and top 10. Useful to flag when assembling a team for a project that needs those strengths.

Bottleneck Talents

Talents that only one or two people in the department carry in their top 5. If those people are unavailable for an assignment, that strength essentially leaves the department - useful to know when scoping a project that needs them, or when thinking about cross-training and resilience.

Distinctiveness

How the score is calculated and how to read the numbers

For each person, distinctiveness is the sum of 1 ÷ (how many people share each top-5 talent), across their five signature strengths. In plain English: rare talents are worth more, common talents are worth less.

How to interpret the score:

  • A score near 5.0 would mean every one of someone's top-5 talents is held by them alone - a one-of-a-kind profile in this department.
  • A score near 1.0 means each of their top-5 talents is widely shared by many colleagues - a very "typical" profile.
  • Realistic scores in this dataset land roughly between 0.5 and 2.5. The higher a person sits in the ranking, the harder it is to replace their unique contribution by reshuffling other team members.

Each chip shows the talent and its frequency in the 23-person department (e.g. "Belief 1/23" means only that one person carries Belief in their top 5 - the rarest possible). The narrower the fraction, the bigger that talent's contribution to the person's distinctiveness score. Chips within a row are sorted with the rarest first.
